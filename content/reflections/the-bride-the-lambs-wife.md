Near the end of the Bible, an angel says to John, "Come hither, I will shew thee the bride, the Lamb's wife" (Revelation 21:9). John is expecting a woman. What he's shown is a city full of people.

This reflection follows that bride from the first wedding in Eden to the last one in Revelation, and through the hard years in between.

## The first wedding

Everything in Genesis 1 is "good," until one thing isn't: "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him" (Genesis 2:18). God causes "a deep sleep to fall upon Adam," takes a rib, makes a woman, "and brought her unto the man" (2:21–22). Adam's first recorded words are a love poem: "This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh" (2:23). Then the narrator adds, "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh" (2:24).

Paul quotes that verse thousands of years later and says something surprising: "This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32).

(my reading) Marriage wasn't invented first and then borrowed as a picture of God's love. It was made as the picture. The first wedding is a preview of the last one.

## A bride sent for

After Moriah, the next story about Isaac is his father sending for a bride. Abraham sends his servant to his homeland with ten camels and "all the goods of his master" (Genesis 24:10). At a well, the servant meets Rebekah. He gives her "jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and raiment" (24:53) and tells her family all about his master's son.

Then comes the question that decides everything. "Wilt thou go with this man? And she said, I will go" (24:58). She has never seen Isaac. She goes on the word of the one who was sent.

The story ends with one of the tenderest lines in Genesis: Isaac "took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her" (24:67).

(my reading) The father sends, the servant speaks only of the son, and the bride says yes to someone she hasn't seen. Later, Jesus describes the Spirit the same way: "he shall not speak of himself... He shall glorify me: for he shall receive of mine, and shall shew it unto you" (John 16:13–14). The text never says Genesis 24 is about the Spirit. It just tells the story in that shape.

## The outsider bride

Ruth is a widow from Moab, an outsider with no claim on Israel. At night she lies at the feet of Boaz and asks him to take her in: "spread therefore thy skirt over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman" (Ruth 3:9). Boaz pays the price at the city gate: "Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon, have I purchased to be my wife" (4:10). She becomes the great-grandmother of David, and her name is in the family line of Jesus (Matthew 1:5).

Centuries later, God uses the same words for himself. "I spread my skirt over thee, and covered thy nakedness: yea, I sware unto thee, and entered into a covenant with thee, saith the Lord GOD, and thou becamest mine" (Ezekiel 16:8). (Pattern: the two passages use the same act. The text doesn't say one is about the other.)

## A wedding in the wilderness

When God brings Israel out of Egypt, he describes it in the language of courtship. "Ye have seen... how I bare you on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself" (Exodus 19:4). Jeremiah later looks back on those days the way a husband remembers the beginning: "I remember thee, the kindness of thy youth, the love of thine espousals, when thou wentest after me in the wilderness" (Jeremiah 2:2).

(my reading) The order matters. First the Passover lamb, then the covenant at Sinai. The rescue comes before the vows.

## The bride who kept leaving

The middle of the story is painful. Israel turns to other gods, and the prophets call it what it is: unfaithfulness in a marriage. In Ezekiel 16, God describes finding Jerusalem abandoned as a newborn, washing her, clothing her, marrying her, and then watching her give herself to others: "But thou didst trust in thine own beauty, and playedst the harlot" (Ezekiel 16:15).

**Honest note:** Ezekiel 16 and 23 are among the most graphic chapters in the Bible. They're hard to read on purpose. They show how betrayal feels from the husband's side.

Hosea is told to live the story out. "Go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress, according to the love of the LORD toward the children of Israel" (Hosea 3:1). He has to pay to get her back: "So I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver" (3:2).

And God doesn't give up on his bride. "I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies" (Hosea 2:19). "Thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (2:16), meaning *my husband*, not *my master*. Isaiah promises the same: "thy Maker is thine husband; the LORD of hosts is his name" (Isaiah 54:5), and "as the bridegroom rejoiceth over the bride, so shall thy God rejoice over thee" (62:5).

The Song of Songs puts it simply: "Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it" (Song of Songs 8:7).

## The bridegroom arrives

When Jesus comes, he steps into the role. His first sign is at a wedding in Cana, where the steward says, "thou hast kept the good wine until now" (John 2:10). John the Baptist calls himself the best man: "He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom's voice" (John 3:29). When people ask why his disciples don't fast, Jesus answers, "Can the children of the bridechamber mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them? but the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken from them" (Matthew 9:15).

On his last night, he talks like a groom before the wedding: "In my Father's house are many mansions... I go to prepare a place for you. And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again, and receive you unto myself" (John 14:2–3). And he tells a story about ten young women waiting with lamps, and a cry at midnight: "Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him" (Matthew 25:6).

## The price of the bride

Hosea paid fifteen pieces of silver. Boaz paid at the gate. The bridegroom pays with his life. "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:25–27).

Paul sees his own work as arranging a marriage: "I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ" (2 Corinthians 11:2).

(my reading) The bride in Hosea and Ezekiel kept leaving. The bride in Ephesians is "not having spot, or wrinkle." Nothing in between changed her except what the bridegroom did.

## The wedding

Then the wedding finally comes. "Let us be glad and rejoice, and give honour to him: for the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready. And to her was granted that she should be arrayed in fine linen, clean and white: for the fine linen is the righteousness of saints" (Revelation 19:7–8). "Blessed are they which are called unto the marriage supper of the Lamb" (19:9).

(my reading) Notice "to her was granted." She makes herself ready, but the dress is given to her. It's the same pattern as Eden, where God replaced fig leaves with coats he made himself.

John sees "the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2), and hears what the wedding means: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people" (21:3).

The last invitation in the Bible comes from the bride herself, speaking together with the Spirit: "And the Spirit and the bride say, Come... And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely" (22:17).

## What this means

(my reading) The bride is not one person. She's everyone God has rescued, gathered into one, "the bride, the Lamb's wife." Jesus said that in the resurrection people "neither marry, nor are given in marriage" (Matthew 22:30). Human marriage doesn't continue there, because the marriage it pictured has arrived.

The story runs from a bride brought to Adam, through a bride who kept leaving, to a bride presented without spot. The constant through all of it is the husband. He went after her in the wilderness, bought her back, paid for her with his life, and now says, "Come."

## Outside: notes from the Hebrew and Greek

*This section comes from outside the KJV text.*

- **Ishi and Baali (Hosea 2:16).** *Ishi* means "my man" or "my husband." *Baali* also means "my husband" or "my master," and it's the name of the god Baal. God wants a name that carries no trace of the idol and no sense of ownership.
- **"The righteousness of saints" (Revelation 19:8)** is plural in Greek, *ta dikaiōmata tōn hagiōn*, "the righteous deeds of the saints." The linen is woven from what they did, and it's still "granted" to her.
- **"Bride"** in Revelation is *nymphē*, the same word John the Baptist uses in "He that hath the bride is the bridegroom" (John 3:29).
