Most of us start the Bible at Genesis 1:1, "In the beginning God created." But the Bible itself points further back than that.

On the night before he died, Jesus prayed to his Father: "thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24). Before there was light or land, before angels or stars, there was a Father and a Son, and love between them. That's the first thing that exists in the whole story. Everything else comes out of it.

## A love that wanted to include us

God didn't make the world because he was lonely or needed something. "Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing" (Acts 17:25). He made it because love of that kind reaches outward. Before anything was made, "he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4). He chose a people to share what the Father and the Son already had.

When the Bible names that goal, it uses the language of a wedding. In Eden, God brings the woman to the man, and "they shall be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Centuries later Paul reads that verse again and says, "This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). The first marriage was a preview. God's plan was a union with his people, as close as a husband and wife, and it would last forever.

## A love that planned for the worst

God also knew what was coming. A creature would rebel, and the humans he loved would follow. The union would break almost as soon as it began.

So before any of it happened, love made a plan to pay for it. Peter says the Lamb was "foreordained before the foundation of the world" (1 Peter 1:20). The rescue wasn't an afterthought. It was in place before the first day. That's why God doesn't look surprised in Genesis 3. On the day Adam and Eve fell, he was already promising that the woman's seed would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15).

God didn't love us only after we were lovable. He loved us knowing exactly what we would do.

## A love that paid the price

The rest of the Old Testament is that love rehearsing what it will cost.

On Mount Moriah, Abraham climbs with his son Isaac, "thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest," the first time the word *love* appears in the Bible. Isaac carries the wood and asks, "where is the lamb?" Abraham answers, "God will provide himself a lamb" (Genesis 22:2, 7–8). At the last moment God stops Abraham's hand. A ram dies instead, and the father keeps his son.

Right after that, Abraham sends his servant to find a bride for the son who came back from the altar. Rebekah has never seen Isaac, but she says, "I will go" (Genesis 24:58).

Then come the prophets, and the love story turns painful. God's people keep leaving him. Hosea is told to marry a woman who will be unfaithful, and later to buy her back, as a picture of what God is doing. God says through him, "I will betroth thee unto me for ever… in lovingkindness, and in mercies" (Hosea 2:19). The bride keeps running, and God keeps coming after her.

At the cross, the Moriah story finishes the way Abraham's didn't. "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). This time no hand stops the knife, and no ram is caught in the thicket. The Son cries, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" so the bride never has to. And "God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8).

That's the love from before the foundation of the world, paying what it planned to pay.

## A love that comes to bring us home

After the resurrection, the Spirit is sent, much like Abraham's servant sent across the desert. He carries the Father's gifts, speaks about the Son, and asks people who have never seen him to come. Paul describes the goal: Christ "loved the church, and gave himself for it… that he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot" (Ephesians 5:25–27).

## A love that finishes what it started

The Bible ends where the love was always heading. "The marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7). John is told, "Come hither, I will shew thee the bride, the Lamb's wife" (21:9). "The tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them" (21:3). The tree of life is open again, and "there shall be no more curse" (22:3).

The last invitation in the book comes from both of them together: "the Spirit and the bride say, Come" (22:17).

## What this means for us

If this is the story, then God's love is older than your worst day and deeper than your worst failure. It was set on you before you existed. It saw what you would do and planned to pay for it anyway. It didn't wait for you to get better. "I have loved thee with an everlasting love" (Jeremiah 31:3). *Everlasting* runs in both directions. The love has no starting point and no end.

The Bible starts with love between a Father and a Son, and it ends with a wedding. Everything in between is love coming to get its bride.
