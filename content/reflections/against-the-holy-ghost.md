The hardest sentence Jesus ever spoke about forgiveness comes right after the widest one. "Verily I say unto you, All sins shall be forgiven unto the sons of men, and blasphemies wherewith soever they shall blaspheme" (Mark 3:28). Every sin, every blasphemy. Then the exception: "But he that shall blaspheme against the Holy Ghost hath never forgiveness, but is in danger of eternal damnation" (3:29).

This reflection follows what happened that day, what the sin is, why it can't be forgiven, and who doesn't need to fear it.

## What happened

A man is brought to Jesus "possessed with a devil, blind, and dumb: and he healed him, insomuch that the blind and dumb both spake and saw" (Matthew 12:22). The crowd starts to say the obvious thing: "Is not this the son of David?" (12:23).

The religious experts have a different answer. "The scribes which came down from Jerusalem said, He hath Beelzebub, and by the prince of the devils casteth he out devils" (Mark 3:22). They can't deny the healing. A blind man sees and a mute man talks, in front of everyone. So they give it a different source.

Jesus answers with plain logic. "How can Satan cast out Satan?... if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand" (Mark 3:23, 25). Then he says what is really happening: "No man can enter into a strong man's house, and spoil his goods, except he will first bind the strong man" (3:27). Satan isn't working through Jesus. Satan is being tied up by him.

Then comes the warning, and Mark adds one line so no one can miss why it was said: "Because they said, He hath an unclean spirit" (Mark 3:30).

## What the sin is

Matthew tells us who was really at work that day. Jesus says, "if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you" (Matthew 12:28). Luke puts it "with the finger of God" (Luke 11:20). The scribes looked at the Holy Spirit freeing a man, and called it the devil.

That's the sin, as the text defines it: seeing God's Spirit do something plainly good and calling it evil. Isaiah described it centuries earlier: "Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness" (Isaiah 5:20).

The text is just as clear about what it isn't.

- **It isn't speaking against Jesus.** "Whosoever speaketh a word against the Son of man, it shall be forgiven him: but whosoever speaketh against the Holy Ghost, it shall not be forgiven him" (Matthew 12:32; Luke 12:10). Rejecting Jesus out of confusion or misunderstanding is forgivable.
- **It isn't blasphemy done in ignorance.** Paul says he "was before a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious: but I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief" (1 Timothy 1:13). He even "compelled them to blaspheme" (Acts 26:11). He was there when Stephen told the council, "ye do always resist the Holy Ghost" (Acts 7:51), and "Saul was consenting unto his death" (Acts 8:1). He was forgiven anyway.
- **It isn't denying Jesus out of fear.** Peter denied him three times. "And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter... And Peter went out, and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:61–62). A few weeks later Jesus asks him three times, "lovest thou me?" and gives him his flock (John 21:17).
- **It isn't thinking Jesus has lost his mind.** In the same chapter his own family "went out to lay hold on him: for they said, He is beside himself" (Mark 3:21). Mark wraps the scribes' story inside the family's story. The family shows up again outside the door a few verses later (3:31). Jesus doesn't warn them. He warns the scribes.

(my reading) The difference between the family and the scribes is the point. The family was worried and wrong. The scribes saw clearly and called it the devil anyway, because the alternative was admitting the kingdom of God had come and they weren't in charge of it.

## Why it can't be forgiven

Jesus doesn't say God is unwilling to forgive this one sin while forgiving murder and adultery. The text points somewhere else: to what the Spirit does.

The Spirit is the one who shows people their sin. "When he is come, he will reprove the world of sin... Of sin, because they believe not on me" (John 16:8–9). Repentance starts there. So does every forgiveness in the Bible.

(my reading) If a person looks at the Spirit's work and calls it Satan's, they've rejected the only one who can lead them to repentance. It isn't that God refuses someone who turns back. It's that the person has refused the one who turns people back. Forgiveness isn't withheld. The road to it has been closed from the inside.

Jesus says the same thing in other words. To the Pharisees who claimed to see: "If ye were blind, ye should have no sin: but now ye say, We see; therefore your sin remaineth" (John 9:41). And: "If I had not done among them the works which none other man did, they had not had sin: but now have they both seen and hated both me and my Father" (John 15:24). The sin that remains is the one committed with open eyes.

Hebrews says it most sharply. For people who "were made partakers of the Holy Ghost" and then fall away, "it is impossible... to renew them again unto repentance" (Hebrews 6:4–6). Later it speaks of someone who "hath done despite unto the Spirit of grace" (10:29), and of Esau, who "found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears" (12:17). John names "a sin unto death" and doesn't tell anyone to pray for it (1 John 5:16).

**Honest note.** None of these passages draws an exact line. Hebrews 6 and 10 are hard to read, and John never says what the sin unto death is. They stand as warnings, and they don't explain themselves further.

## It doesn't come out of nowhere

The Old Testament already has the shape of this sin. Under the law, most sins had a sacrifice. One kind didn't: "the soul that doeth ought presumptuously... the same reproacheth the LORD; and that soul shall be cut off... Because he hath despised the word of the LORD" (Numbers 15:30–31). It describes a defiant sin, done on purpose against what God plainly said.

Isaiah describes the same thing with the Spirit named: "they rebelled, and vexed his holy Spirit: therefore he was turned to be their enemy" (Isaiah 63:10). Isaiah's own commission describes where it ends, a people who "see ye indeed, but perceive not," with hearts made fat, "lest they... convert, and be healed" (Isaiah 6:9–10).

It's a hardening that grows over time. When Jesus healed a withered hand in the synagogue, he "looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts" (Mark 3:5). That's earlier in the same chapter. The Beelzebub charge is where that hardness ends up. Hebrews gives the warning and the cure in one line: "exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin" (Hebrews 3:13).

## Who doesn't need to fear it

(my reading) The people in this story were not worried that they had sinned against the Holy Ghost. They were sure they were right. A person who lies awake afraid they've committed this sin is in the opposite condition: troubled about their sin and wanting God. In the Bible, that trouble is the Spirit's work (John 16:8). The fear itself is a sign that the Spirit is still speaking to them.

The rest of the Bible speaks to that person without conditions:

- "Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37).
- "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9).
- "If our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things" (1 John 3:20).
- "A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench" (Matthew 12:20, quoting Isaiah 42:3). Matthew quotes that a few verses before the Beelzebub story.

At the cross, Jesus prays for the men nailing him there: "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). A few weeks later Peter tells the crowd that crucified him, "I wot that through ignorance ye did it" (Acts 3:17), and calls them to repent. The door stayed open even for them.

## What this means

(my reading) This sin isn't a slip of the tongue or a dark thought someone can't take back. It's a settled choice to look at God's goodness, done in plain view, and call it evil, because accepting it would mean giving up control. It can't be forgiven because it rejects the only one who leads people to forgiveness.

It also fits the thread that runs through the whole Bible. The last invitation in the book is spoken by the Spirit, together with the bride: "And the Spirit and the bride say, Come... And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely" (Revelation 22:17). The one unforgivable sin is calling the voice that says "Come" the devil's. Anyone who still hears it as an invitation hasn't done that.

## Outside: two notes from the Greek

*This section comes from outside the KJV text. It uses the Greek of the New Testament.*

- **"Hath never forgiveness."** The Greek of Mark 3:29 is *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiona*, literally "has no release into the age." *Aphesis* is the ordinary New Testament word for forgiveness. Its basic meaning is release, a debt let go.
- **"Eternal damnation" or "eternal sin."** The manuscripts behind the KJV read "in danger of eternal judgment" (*aioniou kriseos*). Many older Greek manuscripts read "guilty of an eternal sin" (*aioniou hamartematos*). The second reading fits the reading above: the sin stays because the person stays in it.
