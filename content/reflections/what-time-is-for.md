Read from Genesis 1:1 and the Bible starts with the clock already running. Read from John 1:1 and it starts before the clock: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God" (John 1:1). Time shows up later, as something God makes. Following it from there to the last page shows what time is in this book, what it's for, and how it ends.

## Before the clock

The first things the Bible describes happen before time. The Son says, "thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24). Eternal life was "promised before the world began" (Titus 1:2), and grace "was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began" (2 Timothy 1:9). Moses puts God on the far side of creation: "Before the mountains were brought forth… even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God" (Psalm 90:2).

God's own name sits outside the order of past and future. At the bush he calls himself "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14). Jesus uses the same kind of words and breaks ordinary grammar to do it: "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58). He doesn't say "I was." Isaiah calls God the one "that inhabiteth eternity" (Isaiah 57:15). In the same verse he says God also dwells "with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit."

(my reading) So the Bible's first claim about time is that it isn't ultimate. Love, a plan and a promise all exist before it. Time is where they get carried out.

## Time is made, and made for something

On the fourth day God makes the lights "for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years" (Genesis 1:14). Time here is something God makes for a purpose. It measures his world, but it doesn't govern him.

Then comes the seventh day. "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished," and God "rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made" (Genesis 2:1–2). He didn't rest because he was tired. He "fainteth not, neither is weary" (Isaiah 40:28). He rested because the work was done. Time doesn't stop there, either. Days and years keep going right into the next chapter. What begins on the seventh day is God's *rest*, and he "blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it" (Genesis 2:3). The first thing in the Bible called holy is a day, not a place or a person.

Hebrews says that rest is still open. God's works "were finished from the foundation of the world," and yet "There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God" (Hebrews 4:3, 9). People enter it the way God did, by ceasing "from his own works, as God did from his" (4:10), and the time to enter is always the same word: "To day if ye will hear his voice" (4:7).

**Honest note:** each of the first six days closes with "the evening and the morning were the … day." The seventh has no such line. It's tempting to read that as Genesis leaving God's rest open on purpose, and it fits what Hebrews says. But Hebrews never points to the missing line. It gets there from Psalm 95 ("they should not enter into my rest"). The missing evening is an argument from silence, and I hold it loosely.

The Law turns the calendar into a series of meetings. "Concerning the feasts of the LORD… even these are my feasts" (Leviticus 23:2). At the tent, God says, "there I will meet with the children of Israel" (Exodus 29:43). The sabbath is "a sign between me and you throughout your generations" (Exodus 31:13). The week, the new moons and the yearly feasts are dates God has set to be with his people.

## How we lost the rest

The rest didn't end. We left it.

God set the man in the garden "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15), and he warned, "in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die" (2:17). After they eat, the first thing Adam and Eve do is back away: they "hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden" (3:8). Only after that does God send them out, "lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever" (3:22–24). Rest turns into toil: "in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life… In the sweat of thy face" (3:17–19).

Adam doesn't drop dead that day. He lives to 930, "and he died" (5:5). So the death God warned about is more than the body stopping. That day they were cut off from God's presence and from the tree of life, and the body began its road back to dust: "unto dust shalt thou return" (3:19). The Bible keeps both meanings. It calls living people dead, "dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1), and calls the prodigal "dead, and… alive again" (Luke 15:24). Jesus puts both in one sentence: "he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die" (John 11:25–26).

(my reading) So death isn't what ended the rest. Death is what life outside it looks like. People have wanted back in ever since. Lamech names his son Noah hoping "This same shall comfort us concerning our work and toil of our hands" (Genesis 5:29). Israel reaches the land but doesn't enter the rest, "because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19). Then Jesus opens the way: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Even death gets turned around for those in him: "Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord… that they may rest from their labours" (Revelation 14:13).

**Honest note:** that doesn't make physical death good. It's still "the last enemy that shall be destroyed" (1 Corinthians 15:26). It just doesn't get the last word.

How the separation itself happened, step by step, is the subject of its own reflection, "Who Told Thee That Thou Wast Naked?"

## The promise keeps a schedule

Once the promise is made, it runs on God's timetable, and the Bible keeps calling that timetable "the set time." Isaac is born "at the set time of which God had spoken" (Genesis 21:2). Job asks God to "appoint me a set time, and remember me," and says, "all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come" (Job 14:13–14). Habakkuk is told, "the vision is yet for an appointed time… though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come" (Habakkuk 2:3). Daniel is given seventy weeks (Daniel 9:24).

Then the waiting ends. "When the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son" (Galatians 4:4). Jesus opens his preaching with "The time is fulfilled" (Mark 1:15).

In John's Gospel the whole ministry runs toward one hour. At Cana he says, "mine hour is not yet come" (John 2:4). Twice the crowds try to seize him and can't, "because his hour was not yet come" (John 7:30; 8:20). Then in Jerusalem: "The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified" (John 12:23). On the last night he prays, "Father, the hour is come" (John 17:1). "In due time Christ died for the ungodly" (Romans 5:6).

This is where the Bible holds the two sides of time together. The Lamb was "foreordained before the foundation of the world, but was manifest in these last times" (1 Peter 1:20). Revelation calls him "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" (Revelation 13:8). Yet Mark gives the clock times: darkness "until the ninth hour," and then the cry (Mark 15:33–34). (my reading) From God's side the rescue was settled before the first day. From ours it happens on a Friday afternoon. The Bible doesn't treat those as competing accounts. It treats them as the same event, seen from outside time and from inside it.

## Time from the inside

People in the Bible don't experience time as a plan. They feel it as short, and heavy. "Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble" (Job 14:1). "The days of our years are threescore years and ten… for it is soon cut off, and we fly away" (Psalm 90:10). "What is your life? It is even a vapour" (James 4:14).

Ecclesiastes voices the weariness most openly. "One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh" (Ecclesiastes 1:4), and "there is no new thing under the sun" (1:9). Then it adds a line that points past its own complaint: "He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end" (Ecclesiastes 3:11). People can feel that there's a whole story without being able to see it.

The Bible's answer to that isn't more information. It's trust. "My times are in thy hand" (Psalm 31:15). "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom" (Psalm 90:12). Moses prays that line after burying a whole generation in the wilderness.

**Honest note:** the Bible doesn't hide how hard the waiting is. "How long" is one of its most common prayers, from the psalms to Habakkuk ("O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear!" Habakkuk 1:2). It's still being prayed at the end, by the martyrs under the altar: "How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge?" (Revelation 6:10). Their answer is to "rest yet for a little season" (6:11). Not even the saints in heaven are spared the wait.

## Looking forward, looking back

You might expect the Old Testament to show time as people live it and the New Testament to show it as God sees it. The text doesn't split that neatly. Moses had already written "a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday" (Psalm 90:4), and Isaiah heard God "declaring the end from the beginning" (Isaiah 46:10). The apostles, for their part, still didn't know the timetable: "of that day and that hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels which are in heaven, neither the Son, but the Father" (Mark 13:32).

The difference the New Testament names is where the writers stood. The prophets "enquired and searched diligently… what, or what manner of time" their own words pointed to, and they learned "that not unto themselves, but unto us they did minister" (1 Peter 1:10–12). The patriarchs "died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off" (Hebrews 11:13). Daniel says, "I heard, but I understood not," and is told the words are "sealed till the time of the end" (Daniel 12:8–9). Jesus tells his disciples that "many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and have not seen them" (Matthew 13:17).

(my reading) The Old Testament looks forward to a promise whose end it can't see. The New Testament looks back on a promise it watched come true. Both are written from inside time, and both say God stands outside it. The apostles never claim to have stepped outside the clock. They say someone came in from outside it: "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him" (John 1:18). Read in order, you stand in both places. You wait with the prophets before you look back with the apostles.

The two groups also finish together. The faithful of old "received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:39–40).

## Why the wait

Peter takes on the scoffers' question, "Where is the promise of his coming?" He answers from Psalm 90: "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:4, 8). Then he gives the reason for the delay: "The Lord is not slack concerning his promise… but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish" (3:9).

(my reading) The gap between the first coming and the second isn't God running late. It's the door being held open. That's why the New Testament keeps pressing on the present moment: "behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2). "Redeeming the time, because the days are evil" (Ephesians 5:16). The most important time in the Bible's calendar is "To day."

## Why give us time at all

If God is outside time, why make it? The Bible never answers that in a single sentence. Ecclesiastes even says no one "can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end" (Ecclesiastes 3:11). But it does say what time is for, one piece at a time, and the pieces line up.

**It's made for us, not for God.** He "inhabiteth eternity," and he isn't "worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing" (Acts 17:25). Jesus says it directly about the holy day of the week: "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27). Time belongs to God, since "my times are in thy hand" (Psalm 31:15), but he made it for us.

**It came before the fall.** The six days and the blessed seventh are all in Eden, and all of it is "very good" (Genesis 1:31). What the fall added was death: "till thou return unto the ground" (Genesis 3:19), and then Genesis 5's refrain, "and he died." Time wasn't the curse. Running out of it was. That's why the end takes away death and night but keeps the months.

**It's for finding him.** In Athens, Paul says God "determined the times before appointed… That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us" (Acts 17:26–27).

**It's for turning around.** "I gave her space to repent" (Revelation 2:21). "The longsuffering of God waited in the days of Noah" (1 Peter 3:20). "The goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance" (Romans 2:4). God himself chooses to wait: "therefore will the LORD wait, that he may be gracious unto you" (Isaiah 30:18). Nobody can change their mind without a before and an after.

**It's for growing.** "First the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear" (Mark 4:28). We "are changed… from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18). Even the Son took time: "Jesus increased in wisdom and stature" (Luke 2:52), and "though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered" (Hebrews 5:8).

**It's for trusting.** "Hope that is seen is not hope… if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it" (Romans 8:24–25). A promise needs a future to be kept in, and faithfulness only shows over time. God "keepeth covenant and mercy… to a thousand generations" (Deuteronomy 7:9), and his mercies "are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23).

**It's for remembering.** The Passover is "for a memorial… throughout your generations" (Exodus 12:14). At the last supper Jesus says, "this do in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19).

**It's for filling the house.** The master sends his servant out again "that my house may be filled" (Luke 14:23), and "this gospel of the kingdom shall be preached in all the world… and then shall the end come" (Matthew 24:14).

(my reading) Put together, time is the room God made for a relationship to happen in. Love that's chosen, promises that are kept, trust that's tested, people who are found and then grow up: none of that can happen in an instant. Time doesn't give God anything he lacks. It gives his creatures room to become his people. And God didn't just hand time to us. He came into it, "made of a woman, made under the law" (Galatians 4:4), and waited for his hour like everyone else.

Paul names three things that last: "faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity" (1 Corinthians 13:13). (my reading) Faith and hope both need a "not yet," so they only make sense inside time. Love is the one the Bible places before time ("thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world") and after it. Time is where love waits to be answered.

**Honest note:** this section is a synthesis. No verse says "God made time so that…" Each purpose above is stated about one part of time: a day, a season, a delay. The picture of the whole is my reading of them together.

## How time ends

In the KJV, an angel swears in Revelation 10:6 "that there should be time no longer." It's easy to read that as the end of time itself. The next verse makes the point more specific: "the mystery of God should be finished, as he hath declared to his servants the prophets" (10:7). The waiting is what ends. (The Greek, below, makes this plainer still.)

What the last chapters take away is the darkness and the loss, not every kind of time: "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying" (Revelation 21:4); "there shall be no night there" (21:25); "the Lord God giveth them light" (22:5). Isaiah saw it first: "Thy sun shall no more go down… the days of thy mourning shall be ended" (Isaiah 60:20).

**Honest note:** the Bible doesn't describe the new creation as a frozen, timeless present. The tree of life "yielded her fruit every month" (Revelation 22:2). Isaiah says worship goes on "from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another" (Isaiah 66:23). The text is plainest about the kinds of time that end: night, death, delay and mourning. On whether anything like sequence continues, it says less. Some people will take these verses as pictures, and they may be. But the text doesn't say "timeless," and this reflection doesn't either.

## What this means

(my reading) Put in order, the Bible tells a consistent story about time. God doesn't need it. He made it for us, as room to seek him, turn, grow, trust and be found. It was made on the fourth day for "seasons," and the Law filled those seasons with appointed meetings. The promise travels through it one set time after another until "the fulness of the time." We live inside it, where days are few and waiting is hard, and we're told that our times are in his hand and the delay is mercy. It ends at a wedding, where "the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3). There, meeting with God is no longer something on the calendar. It just goes on.

Time began at creation and it keeps running. What the story opens with is God resting in a finished work. What it closes with is people finally sharing that rest, in a city with "no night there."

## Outside: what the Hebrew and Greek add

*Everything in this section comes from outside the KJV text. It checks the Hebrew and Greek behind the verses above. The Hebrew is the Westminster Leningrad Codex, and the Greek is the SBL Greek New Testament, both word-tagged. Word counts are from those tagged texts. The KJV's New Testament was translated from an older printed Greek text (the Textus Receptus), which explains the two differences noted near the end.*

The short answer is that the original languages don't change the story. They sharpen it in several places. Two KJV renderings shift the meaning. A few "everlasting"s are weaker in Hebrew than in English. One Greek distinction is often overstated.

### Changes the meaning

- **"Time no longer" (Revelation 10:6)** is *chronos ouketi estai*, "there shall be no more *chronos*." Six chapters earlier the martyrs were told to rest *chronon mikron*, "a little *chronos*" (6:11, KJV "a little season"). The same word links the two. The martyrs asked "how long," were told to wait a little longer, and here the waiting is over. Most modern translations render 10:6 "no more delay." It's an announcement that God has stopped waiting, not a claim that time itself ceases.
- **"He hath set the world in their heart" (Ecclesiastes 3:11)** uses *ha'olam*, the Hebrew word the KJV usually renders "for ever" or "everlasting." Many translations read it "he has put eternity into man's heart." Read that way, the verse says people can sense the whole of time from beginning to end but can't see it.

### Sharpens the meaning

- **"Seasons" (Genesis 1:14)** is *mo'adim*, "appointed times." That's the same word the KJV renders "feasts" in "the feasts of the LORD" (Leviticus 23:2). It's also the word inside "the tabernacle of the congregation," *ohel mo'ed*, the tent of meeting. And it shares a root with the verb in "there I will meet with the children of Israel" (Exodus 29:42–43). *Mo'ed* appears 223 times, and the KJV renders it "congregation" in about 135 of the verses where it appears. On day four, the lights were made to mark God's appointed meetings with his people.
- **"For ever" and "everlasting"** usually translate *'olam* (438 times). The word means the far horizon of time, as far as anyone can see. Its length depends on what it describes. A servant serves his master *le'olam*, "for ever," meaning for life (Exodus 21:6). Jonah says the earth's bars were about him *le'olam* (Jonah 2:6), and he was in the fish three days. When *'olam* describes God, the context supplies the endlessness: "thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end" (Psalm 102:27).
- **"Inhabiteth eternity" (Isaiah 57:15)** is *shokhen 'ad*, "dwelling in perpetuity." The same verb comes back in "I dwell… with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit," and it's the root of *mishkan*, the tabernacle. The Greek has the same pairing: "the Word… dwelt among us" (John 1:14) and "he will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3) both use *skēnoō*, "to tent." The God who dwells in eternity also dwells with humble people.
- **"I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14)** is *ehyeh asher ehyeh*, the verb "to be" in a form that also means "I will be." Two verses earlier the same word *ehyeh* makes a promise: "Certainly I will be with thee" (3:12). The name looks forward and promises presence.
- **"Before the world began" (Titus 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:9)** is *pro chronōn aiōniōn*, "before times eternal," or before the ages of time. The promise is said to predate time itself. 1 Corinthians 2:7 says the same with "before the ages."
- **"World" often means "age."** The KJV renders *aiōn* as "world" in about 40 verses. "The end of the world" (Matthew 24:3; 28:20) is "the completion of the age." "By whom also he made the worlds" (Hebrews 1:2) is literally "he made the ages," and Hebrews 11:3 is the same. And "I am with you alway" (Matthew 28:20) is literally "I am with you all the days."
- **"Eternal life"** is *zōē aiōnios*, "the life of the age to come." Jesus defines it by a relationship, not a length: "this is life eternal, that they might know thee" (John 17:3). And it starts now. The believer "hath everlasting life" (John 5:24), in the present tense.
- **"Which is to come" (Revelation 1:8)** is *ho erchomenos*, "the one who is coming," not "the one who will be." God's future is described as his arrival.
- **"Before" and "after."** The Hebrew *qedem* means "in front," "east" and "of old." Psalm 139:5 has "behind and before," *achor vaqedem*. "The end" in "declaring the end from the beginning" (Isaiah 46:10) is *acharit*, from the root for "behind" or "after." In this vocabulary the past is what lies in front of you and the future is what comes behind.
- **"The first day" (Genesis 1:5)** is literally "day one," *yom echad*. The other days get ordinals: second, third and so on. The phrase *yom echad* comes back in Zechariah 14:7 for the last day: "it shall be one day which shall be known to the LORD, not day, nor night: but… at evening time it shall be light." (Pattern.)

### Weaker in Hebrew than in the KJV

- **Micah 5:2**, "whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting," is literally "from *qedem*, from the days of *'olam*." The same phrase, "the days of *'olam*," is "the days of old" in Micah 7:14 and Amos 9:11. The verse fits eternal origins, and the New Testament's "before the world was" (John 17:5) supplies that. But Micah's Hebrew alone could also mean "from ancient days."
- **Habakkuk 1:12**, "Art thou not from everlasting," is *miqqedem*, "from of old." It's the same word as in "The eternal God is thy refuge" (Deuteronomy 33:27), literally "the God of old."

### Overstated

- **Chronos and kairos.** It's often said that *chronos* means clock time and *kairos* means the right moment. There's something to it. "The time is fulfilled" (Mark 1:15), "the fulness of times" (Ephesians 1:10), "in due time Christ died" (Romans 5:6), "now is the accepted time" (2 Corinthians 6:2) and "redeeming the time" (Ephesians 5:16) all use *kairos*. But "when the fulness of the time was come" (Galatians 4:4) uses *chronos*, and Acts 1:7 puts the two side by side: "the times or the seasons." The Greek words overlap too much to carry the point by themselves. The appointed-moment idea comes from the text, not the vocabulary.

### Where the Greek text itself differs

- **Revelation 11:17.** The KJV reads "which art, and wast, and art to come." The older Greek manuscripts have only "which art, and wast," and the verse gives the reason: "because thou hast taken to thee thy great power, and hast reigned." (my reading) At that point in the vision, he's no longer "to come," because he has come.
- **Revelation 16:5.** The KJV reads "which art, and wast, and shalt be." The Greek manuscripts read "which art, and wast, the Holy One."
