Partway up a mountain, a boy asks his father a question. "Behold the fire and the wood: but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" (Genesis 22:7).

It's one of the oldest questions in the Bible, and one of the last to be answered. This reflection follows it from Moriah to the throne.

## The question on Moriah

God had told Abraham, "Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest, and get thee into the land of Moriah; and offer him there for a burnt offering" (Genesis 22:2). Isaac carries the wood. Abraham carries "the fire in his hand, and a knife; and they went both of them together" (22:6).

When Isaac asks about the lamb, Abraham answers, "My son, God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering" (22:8). Then God stops the knife, and Abraham sees "behind him a ram caught in a thicket by his horns" (22:13).

(my reading) Look closely at what happens. Abraham promised a lamb. God provided a ram. Isaac comes down the mountain alive, but his question hasn't been answered yet. Abraham seems to know it. He names the place "Jehovahjireh: as it is said to this day, In the mount of the LORD it shall be seen" (22:14). That's future tense: something is still to be seen on this mountain.

## A lamb for every house

Centuries later, the question gets a partial answer in Egypt. "They shall take to them every man a lamb, according to the house of their fathers, a lamb for an house" (Exodus 12:3). "Your lamb shall be without blemish, a male of the first year" (12:5). Its blood goes on the door: "when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you" (12:13). Not one of its bones is to be broken (12:46).

So now the lamb has a shape. It has to be spotless. It dies in someone's place, and its blood marks a house that death passes over.

But it's a lamb every year, never the lamb. And the law adds more: "two lambs of the first year day by day continually," one in the morning and one at evening (Exodus 29:38–39). Generation after generation, Israel kept bringing lambs.

## Back to the same mountain

The temple, where those lambs were offered, wasn't built just anywhere. "Solomon began to build the house of the LORD at Jerusalem in mount Moriah" (2 Chronicles 3:1). For about a thousand years, the morning and evening lambs died on the mountain where Abraham had said, "God will provide."

(my reading) The whole sacrificial system stood on the place where Isaac's question was asked and never fully answered. Every lamb was a way of saying *not yet*.

Hebrews says it outright: "it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins" (Hebrews 10:4). The lambs were real, and so was God's acceptance of them. But they were pictures. They pointed at something they couldn't be.

## The lamb becomes a person

Then the prophets start talking about the lamb as a person. Isaiah describes a servant who "was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth" (Isaiah 53:7). Why? "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:6).

(my reading) That's the turn. The lamb for a burnt offering isn't going to be an animal. It's going to be someone who carries what the sheep did.

## "Behold the Lamb of God"

Seven hundred years after Isaiah, a man by the Jordan sees Jesus coming and says, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). The next day he says it again: "Behold the Lamb of God!" (1:36).

Isaac's question finally gets its answer, and it's bigger than the question. Not a lamb for one family or one house or one year, but a lamb that "taketh away the sin of the world."

Later, an official from Ethiopia is riding home, reading Isaiah 53 aloud: "He was led as a sheep to the slaughter; and like a lamb dumb before his shearer, so opened he not his mouth" (Acts 8:32). He asks, "of whom speaketh the prophet this? of himself, or of some other man?" (8:34). Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (8:35).

## The lamb on the cross

The Gospels set the cross on the Passover calendar. Pilate brings Jesus out on "the preparation of the passover" (John 19:14), the day the lambs were being killed. Jesus goes out "bearing his cross" (19:17), carrying the wood as Isaac did. Before Pilate, the one who could have answered every charge "answered him to never a word" (Matthew 27:14), as Isaiah said.

When the soldiers come to break the legs of the crucified men, they find him already dead and don't break his. John explains why that matters: "these things were done, that the scripture should be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken" (John 19:36). That's the Passover rule.

Paul puts it in five words: "Christ our passover is sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7). Peter says we were redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot: Who verily was foreordained before the foundation of the world" (1 Peter 1:19–20).

(my reading) On Moriah, God stopped Abraham's hand and spared the son. At the cross, nothing stopped it. "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). This time there was no ram in the thicket, because this time the Son was the lamb.

## The lamb on the throne

The question's last appearance is in heaven. John is told to look for "the Lion of the tribe of Juda" who "hath prevailed" (Revelation 5:5). He turns and sees something else: "in the midst of the throne... stood a Lamb as it had been slain" (5:6).

(my reading) A lion is announced, and a lamb appears. He wins by being sacrificed, and he still bears the marks.

All of heaven sings, "Worthy is the Lamb that was slain to receive power, and riches, and wisdom, and strength, and honour, and glory, and blessing" (5:12). Then the lamb does something lambs don't do. He leads: "the Lamb which is in the midst of the throne shall feed them, and shall lead them unto living fountains of waters: and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes" (7:17).

At the very end, the city has no temple, and no lambs are offered there anymore, "for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it" (21:22). It needs no sun, "for the glory of God did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof" (21:23). The river of life flows "out of the throne of God and of the Lamb" (22:1).

## What this means

(my reading) A boy asked where the lamb was, and his father said God would provide. Everything after that, the Passover doors, the morning and evening offerings, the temple on the same mountain, Isaiah's silent servant, keeps that question open. John by the Jordan closes it. Revelation shows what the answer turns into: the lamb who was slain is the one on the throne, the light of the city, and the shepherd of his people.

Abraham said, "God will provide himself a lamb." He did. And it cost him what it didn't cost Abraham.

**Honest note:** some readers take Abraham's words to mean "God will provide himself *as* the lamb." It's a beautiful thought, and the rest of the Bible fits it. But the sentence itself means God will provide a lamb for himself, and that's how it should be read. The point stands without the wordplay.

## Outside: notes from the Hebrew and Greek

*This section comes from outside the KJV text.*

- **"Provide."** The Hebrew in Genesis 22:8 and 22:14 is *yir'eh*, literally "will see." "God will see for himself the lamb." The place name means "the LORD will see," and the saying "In the mount of the LORD it shall be seen" uses the same verb. On Moriah, providing and seeing are one word.
- **"Lamb" in Genesis 22 and Exodus 12** is *seh*, a young animal of the flock, sheep or goat. The ram God provides is a different word, *ayil*.
- **Two Greek words.** John the Baptist's "Lamb of God" is *amnos*, the same word as in Isaiah 53:7 in the Greek Old Testament, and in Acts 8:32 and 1 Peter 1:19. Revelation uses a different word throughout, *arnion*. It's the word the risen Jesus uses when he tells Peter, "Feed my lambs" (John 21:15).
