The Bible's second chapter ends with a sentence that's easy to read past: "And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25). Nothing to hide, from each other or from God. One chapter later they're sewing fig leaves and hiding in the trees.

This reflection follows how that happened, step by step, and what God does about it.

## It started with a question about God

The serpent doesn't start with the fruit. He starts with God: "Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?" (Genesis 3:1). Then he contradicts God outright, "Ye shall not surely die," and questions his motive: "For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods" (3:4–5).

(my reading) The first break isn't a bite. It's a new thought: *God is holding out on me.* Once that's believed, the eating is only a matter of time.

Then the woman "saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise" (3:6). Every one of those reasons is true, and none of them involves God. She eats, and gives to her husband "with her; and he did eat."

## What they gained

They weren't unaware before this. Adam had named every living creature (2:19–20), and they both spoke, reasoned and understood what God had told them. What they gained wasn't awareness. It was a particular kind of knowing: "knowing good and evil."

The strange part is that the serpent's promise partly came true. God himself says afterward, "Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil" (3:22).

Knowing good and evil isn't bad in itself. Elsewhere in the Bible it's the mark of growing up. Little children "had no knowledge between good and evil" (Deuteronomy 1:39). Mature believers "have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil" (Hebrews 5:14). Solomon asks God for it outright, "that I may discern between good and bad," and "the speech pleased the Lord" (1 Kings 3:9–10).

So the difference is how they got it. Solomon asked. Adam and Eve took it against God's word, because it was "to be desired to make one wise." (my reading) They didn't just learn what good and evil are. They made themselves the ones who decide. The rest of the Bible keeps naming where that leads: "every man did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25), and "Be not wise in thine own eyes: fear the LORD, and depart from evil" (Proverbs 3:7).

(my reading) There's also a difference in *how* they came to know evil. God knows evil the way a judge does, from outside it. They came to know it by joining in.

## Shame

"And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons" (Genesis 3:7).

Their bodies didn't change. At the end of chapter 2 they were naked and unashamed. Now they're the same two people, seeing themselves with different eyes. The first thing they do is cover up from *each other*, before God has even appeared.

(my reading) They had just made themselves judges of good and evil, and the first people they judged were themselves. They didn't like what they saw. Shame is that self-verdict: *I'm exposed, and I'm not okay.*

Then they hear God coming. "Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden" (3:8). God calls, "Where art thou?" and Adam answers, "I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself" (3:9–10). Fear has replaced trust.

God's reply is the heart of the chapter: "Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?" (3:11). God didn't tell them they were shameful. The shame came from inside them, and God treats it as a symptom of what they did, not as his verdict on them.

## Blame, and a world that pushes back

Asked what happened, Adam says, "The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat" (3:12). He blames his wife, and God for giving her to him. She blames the serpent: "The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat" (3:13).

The bond between them bends: "thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee" (3:16). The ground turns against them: "cursed is the ground for thy sake… Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth" (3:17–18). Only then does the distance become a place. God "drove out the man," and cherubim with "a flaming sword which turned every way" guard the way back to the tree of life (3:24).

So the separation runs from the inside out. Trust breaks first, then shame, then hiding, then blame, and only last, exile. Isaiah later says who built the wall: "your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you" (Isaiah 59:2). God didn't move away first. They did.

## What God does in the middle of it

God comes looking. "Where art thou?" isn't a request for information. It's a call to come out. Jesus later describes his own mission the same way: "the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).

God makes a promise. Before a word is said about thorns or sweat, God tells the serpent that the woman's seed "shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel" (Genesis 3:15).

And God covers them. "Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them" (3:21). He replaces their fig leaves with something he made himself. (my reading) Skins mean something died. The text doesn't say so, but it's the first hint that a real covering costs a life.

## The barrier becomes a curtain

The cherubim at Eden's gate show up again. When Israel builds the tabernacle, the curtain before the Most Holy Place is woven "with cherubims" (Exodus 26:31), and "the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy" (26:33). For centuries Israel lived with the barrier from Eden stitched into cloth. (Pattern: the text puts the two side by side. It never says in so many words that the veil is Eden's gate.)

Then, at the cross, "the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom" (Matthew 27:51). It tore from the top, God's side. Hebrews explains what that opened: "Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh" (Hebrews 10:19–20).

## God's answer to shame is to cover it

From Eden on, the Bible keeps answering shame with clothing:

- "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered" (Psalm 32:1).
- "He hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, he hath covered me with the robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom decketh himself with ornaments, and as a bride adorneth herself with her jewels" (Isaiah 61:10).
- "Fear not; for thou shalt not be ashamed… thou shalt forget the shame of thy youth" (Isaiah 54:4).

At the cross the soldiers "took his garments, and made four parts" (John 19:23). Jesus hung exposed. He "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2). (my reading) He took on the exposure Adam and Eve ran from, so that the ones he covers never have to run.

Then the promise that reverses "I was afraid, because I was naked": "Whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed" (Romans 10:11). John tells believers to "abide in him; that, when he shall appear, we may have confidence, and not be ashamed before him at his coming" (1 John 2:28).

At the end, the risen Lord offers "white raiment, that thou mayest be clothed, and that the shame of thy nakedness do not appear" (Revelation 3:18). The bride is "arrayed in fine linen, clean and white" (19:8). And the hiding in Genesis 3:8 is finally undone: "they shall see his face" (22:4).

## What this means

(my reading) The first separation didn't start with God pulling away. It started with doubting his heart. From there came shame, hiding, blame and exile. God's answer has the same shape run backwards. He comes looking, he promises, he covers, he tears the curtain from his side, and in the end he brings his people close enough to see his face.

The question God asked in the garden is still the right one for anyone living with shame: "Who told thee that thou wast naked?" The Bible's answer is that it wasn't God. He's the one who came with clothes.

## Outside: three notes from the Hebrew

*This section comes from outside the KJV text. It uses the Hebrew of the Westminster Leningrad Codex.*

- **Naked and crafty.** "Naked" in Genesis 2:25 is *arummim*. "Subtil" (crafty), describing the serpent in the very next verse, is *arum*. The two words sound almost identical but come from different roots. The couple is *arummim*, open with nothing to hide. The serpent is *arum*, crafty with everything to hide. The first story of sin opens with a pun about openness and hiding.
- **The first lie.** God said, "thou shalt surely die," *mot tamut*, literally "dying, you shall die" (Genesis 2:17). The serpent says, "Ye shall not surely die," *lo mot temutun* (3:4). It's God's own phrase with a "not" in front. The first lie in the Bible is God's word with one word added.
- **"Know."** The Hebrew *yada* means knowing by experience, not only by information. It's the same word in "Adam knew Eve his wife" (4:1). That fits the reading above: they came to know evil by taking part in it.
